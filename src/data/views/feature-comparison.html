Where each wins

Published receipts measure three of these. The numbers are dated and reproducible (Performance):

tree-sitter wins
  • Raw throughput on deterministic grammars: still 1.35× ahead (C automaton 0.031 µs/byte vs the VM's 0.0418) — the gap closed from 37× at the 1.5.1 baseline, published with every step
  • Incremental editing: faster in absolute terms (µs-scale), where parsanol's splice wins 2.1–16.6× against its own fresh parse
  • Windowed nTED on the corruption site: 0.023 vs 0.059 (the ERROR-node-hole-punch shape)
  • The editor-integration ecosystem (the category it defined)
parsanol wins
  • Diagnostic precision held outright: ±24,744 vs ±56,349 mean diagnostic distance; truncation at ±1 byte where tree-sitter drops a root-ERROR at 0; 0/60 null recoveries
  • 47× over ANTLR 4.13 (python3 target) on the same corpus, 8.8× over Lark LALR
  • Grammar-valid LLM masks at 100% validity with free backtracking — the FSM compilers' blind spot
  • Everything in the matrix's top rows — the artifact contract no competitor has
  • Write once, parse identically everywhere: the corpus enforces it, the receipts prove it

The through-line: tree-sitter optimizes reparsing edited text fast (its wins are the editor's wins). PARG optimizes a grammar as verified data — compiled once, distributed with integrity receipts, parsed identically wherever it lands, consumed by models, decoders, and the string side alike. The throughput race is now a 1.35× question instead of a 37× one, and every step that closed it merged with byte-identical trees — the speed never changed the parse. Those are different products; the receipts say where each stands.

The decoders are consumers, not competitors

XGrammar, llguidance, GBNF, and friends are inference-side constrained-decoding engines: they consume grammars and need them portable, valid, and cheap to lower. PARG exports to them from the artifact (parg export --target xgrammar|llguidance), with unmappable constructs rejected loudly at export time. The grammar language that feeds the decoders is the layer above them — which is why the artifact contract, not decoder throughput, is the comparison that matters.

Decision rules

your problem reach for
A format grammar (configs, DSLs, identifiers, documents) consumed by several languages or teams PARG — the artifact is the point
An editor that repaints syntax on every keystroke over one language tree-sitter — incremental is its reason to exist
A DSL whose consumption is LLM structured output PARG — write the grammar once, export to the decoder
Corrupted production input where "where did it break" matters PARG — declared resync + deepest-position diagnostics
One Rust binary, no artifact distribution, max raw speed pest or a hand-rolled parser — the artifact buys you nothing you need
A grammar shipped as a dependency to other projects PARG — checksummed, receipted, semver-classified by parg diff
